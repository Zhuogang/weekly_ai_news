**1. Just-in-Time Memory: summarize when you know the question**  
*Paper · September 23; surfaced September 24*

Most agent-memory systems summarize a completed task immediately. That asks the summarizer to predict which details some unknown future task will need. Once discarded, those details are difficult to recover.

Salesforce’s **JitMem** retains raw trajectories. At retrieval time, a curator combines relevant traces with the current task and produces a compact, targeted memory. Training becomes simpler: reward the curator using success on the task consuming its output.

With a Qwen3-8B executor, the authors report success rates of **77.4% versus 61.2% on ALFWorld**, and **32.8% versus 16.5% on WebShop**, against the compared learned memory baseline. An untrained curator already helps; RL adds further gains. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.27334v1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The practical appeal is immediate. For FV hunting, an old case might contain useful package-size evidence, a failed search query, or a retailer-specific naming convention. A generic “lessons learned” summary may preserve the wrong thing.

The cost question remains open: executor-token savings do not automatically establish lower total latency or cost once retrieval and curation are included. Results also depend on task order and available historical experience.

**Availability:** [paper and experiments](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.27334v1). I could not verify a public implementation or trained curator checkpoint.

**2. WROP: teach video models that hidden objects still exist**  
*Paper and dataset · September 23; surfaced September 25*

A video can look convincing while quietly deleting an object behind a screen. **World Reasoning with Object Permanence—WROP—** isolates that failure.

The authors build **150 Blender task generators**, covering occlusion, concealed objects moving inside containers, barriers, support removal, and collisions. They release 1.5 million training examples and a 300-question exam. Appearance varies while the underlying physical challenge remains controlled.

Their fine-tuned PWM-WROP ranks first among continuation models and third overall in a blind human comparison across 14 systems. But several comparison models regenerate or edit footage rather than truly continue it, making the within-category result easier to interpret. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.28654v1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The biggest qualification: training and evaluation use the same generator inventory. This supports learning these task families; it does not establish broad physical generalization. The motion is also hand-authored animation rather than an independent physics simulation.

For your video and shelf work, I’d borrow the experimental design: test whether an identity survives occlusion, viewpoint changes, and reappearance, separately from ordinary frame accuracy.

**Availability:** [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28654), [generators and training stack](https://github.com/hokindeng/object-permanence), and [weights](https://huggingface.co/Hokin/PWM-WROP). The repository provides scene metadata and trajectories alongside clips—particularly useful for diagnosing failures. Its data-factory portion uses a non-commercial license; the training-stack portion has separate Cosmos terms. ([github.com](https://github.com/hokindeng/object-permanence?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**3. WhatWorkedBench: did the agent discover a good setting—or understand the experiment?**  
*Benchmark paper · September 23; surfaced September 24*

Imagine an agent tries eight combinations of retrieval, reranking, and verification settings and finds a strong configuration. Ask what happens if you disable reranking under a different verifier, and its explanation may be nonsense.

**WhatWorkedBench** separates optimization from experimental understanding. Agents inspect code, choose a limited number of measurements, then predict scores for all component configurations. Exhaustive CPU execution supplies reference outcomes across **36 tasks, 30 data sources, and eight workflow types**.

One telling result: fitting a Gaussian process to the **same observations collected by agents** raises the authors’ effect-recovery metric from **0.632 to 0.698** in one cohort and **0.621 to 0.720** in another. The evidence already contained information the agent failed to extract. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27490?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That suggests a useful division of labor: let an LLM inspect code and propose experiments, while a numerical model estimates response surfaces and uncertainty.

The boundary is important. These are bounded, exhaustively enumerable workflows. Large training runs introduce stochasticity, continuous parameters, and much more expensive interactions.

**Availability:** [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.27490v1), including protocols and task inventory. I could not verify a public executable release.

For a technical lead, this is an excellent meeting question: **“What can we now predict that we could not predict before these experiments?”**

**4. Rufus-Air: a post-training report that includes the plumbing**  
*Technical report · September 24*

Amazon’s **Rufus-Air** documents eight sequential stages on GLM-4.5-Air-Base: supervised fine-tuning, reasoning RL, coding RL, instruction-following RL, three agent stages, and RLHF.

Its organizing idea is **reward reliability**. Start with capabilities that can be checked relatively cleanly; introduce more easily gamed preference signals later. Filter examples to keep training focused on tasks the current policy can learn from.

The engineering details deserve attention: preserve exact token sequences across multi-turn rollouts, keep chat templates consistent, retain sandbox state throughout coding trajectories, and isolate CPU-heavy environment work from other rollout processing.

The authors report beating the official GLM-4.5-Air release on every reported benchmark except creative writing. They also explicitly acknowledge that the stage order was not proven optimal and that no single evaluation suite was tracked across every stage. ([arxiv.org](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29421v1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

“Accessible” remains relative: RL stages used **8–32 nodes**, and agent environments add substantial operating costs.

**Availability:** [full report](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29421v1), with data sources, configurations, and infrastructure descriptions. I could not verify a standalone Rufus-Air checkpoint or complete project release.

My takeaway: before proposing a new RL algorithm, check whether your rollout serialization, environment lifecycle, and reward computation faithfully implement the existing one.

**5. Julia 1: a small model for choosing, routing, and scoring**  
*New model/runtime release · September 25–26*

Supersonic Labs’ **Julia 1** takes a state, a question, and **2–20 candidate answers**, then scores the options. It adapts the multilingual mmBERT-small encoder into a 144.3M-parameter decision model. Python CPU inference is supported; weights and runtime are public, while the training pipeline is private.

The release reports **71.50% scenario-classification accuracy across 52 MASSIVE locales**. Smaller pilots score 94/100 on AG News and 86/100 on Emotion. Banking77 is weaker: 64/100 using a shortlist, below the supplied comparison reference. Those 100-example pilots are preliminary, and the reference model was not rerun by the authors. ([huggingface.co](https://huggingface.co/SupersonicLabs/Julia-1?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

I like the scope discipline. Many production decisions already have explicit choices: request another image, run OCR, search again, or escalate. Generating free-form text adds work when the output is a branch selection.

For your pipeline, this could be a **text-based routing experiment** using extracted evidence. It does not replace visual verification, and probability calibration would need separate testing.

**Availability:** [model and CPU runtime](https://huggingface.co/SupersonicLabs/Julia-1), plus an [ONNX/WebGPU package](https://huggingface.co/SupersonicLabs/Julia-1-ONNX). The browser package downloads roughly 551 MB of weights and keeps a warmed session resident. That makes model loading and steady-state inference separate deployment concerns. Its WebGPU export does not by itself establish performance on your ARM CPU/ORT environment. ([huggingface.co](https://huggingface.co/SupersonicLabs/Julia-1-ONNX?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**6. Reproducing OLMo 3 in MaxText: the best engineering read this issue**  
*Google engineering report · September 24*

Google reproduced OLMo 3 7B pretraining and mid-training in JAX/MaxText on TPUs, using AI2’s public PyTorch/GPU run as a reference.

The most memorable event: **held-out evaluations exposed a data-loader bug that made training appear better than the reference.** The apparent improvement was memorization.

Their verification goes beyond matching loss: converted-checkpoint parity, held-out perplexity, downstream evaluations, and checkpoint-resume tests. They also describe recovering from host failure and continuing after losing three-quarters of their training capacity while preserving the global batch.

A hardware experiment changed attention from 32 heads of dimension 128 to 16 heads of dimension 256. It ran **12.4% faster**, with matching loss through 120 billion tokens; the full reproduction retained the original architecture. ([developers.googleblog.com](https://developers.googleblog.com/reproducing-olmo-3-7b-pre-training-in-maxtext-case-study-of-large-scale-training-on-tpus/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The careful distinction: resuming the same run can reproduce logged metrics exactly; independently trained models need not produce identical token distributions.

**Read:** [engineering walkthrough](https://developers.googleblog.com/reproducing-olmo-3-7b-pre-training-in-maxtext-case-study-of-large-scale-training-on-tpus/) and [training launcher](https://github.com/AI-Hypercomputer/maxtext/blob/main/src/maxtext/trainers/pre_train/scripts/olmo/run_olmo3_7b_stage1.sh).

This is a strong template for evaluating any framework or hardware migration: define equivalence before optimizing throughput. The authors’ reproduction is substantive evidence, though not an outside audit.

**7. BioNeMo’s MoE tutorial: inspect what the 2.21× actually includes**  
*Technical tutorial · September 24*

This complements the earlier JAX MoE article with a concrete **PyTorch/BioNeMo recipe**.

The tutorial moves expert computation from separate launches into grouped matrix operations, then combines low-precision execution with fused activation and routing-weight operations. Avoiding intermediate tensors matters alongside faster matrix multiplication.

NVIDIA reports **2.21× throughput** on eight B200 GPUs. The breakdown is more informative than the headline:

| Configuration | Tokens/sec/GPU |
|---|---:|
| Hugging Face, BF16, FSDP8 | 4,096 |
| Transformer Engine, BF16, FSDP8 | 4,447 |
| Transformer Engine, expert parallelism, MXFP8 | 9,050 |

The large gain combines **precision and parallelism changes**. It cannot be attributed entirely to replacing one kernel. The fused MXFP8 path also requires Blackwell hardware. ([developer.nvidia.com](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/efficient-moe-training-for-biological-foundation-models/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

**Read:** [tutorial and linked recipe](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/efficient-moe-training-for-biological-foundation-models/). The article includes launch and checkpoint guidance; I could not independently inspect the linked recipe directory.

For skill development, the transferable habit is to draw the full execution path—routing, dispatch, expert computation, combine—and identify which tensors and launches disappear after optimization. “Uses fewer FLOPs” is only the beginning of the performance argument.

2. **[Just-in-Time Memory](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.27334v1)** — my first choice for a practical agent experiment. The central idea is easy to test before investing in training.

**One concrete experiment: does task-specific memory improve FV hunting?**

This is my proposed application of JitMem, not a result established by the paper.

Take a chronological collection of completed, human-validated FV searches. For each later case, allow access only to earlier traces and compare:

- Your current workflow without historical memory.
- A fixed summary written when each earlier case finished.
- Raw historical traces summarized specifically for the current case.

Keep the retrieval method, downstream model, and memory-token budget fixed. Include curation costs in total latency and cost. Measure **correct matches at a fixed false-accept rate**, human-review demand, and whether remembered evidence actually belongs to the current product.

Include difficult cases where the brand matches but size, pack count, or regional packaging differs. Historical experience should help decide *where to look*; it should not substitute for evidence about the present item.

The discussion question: **Are we losing useful knowledge because we summarize too early—or because we retrieve the wrong past experience?**
