# AI Research & Engineering Digest
**November 15, 2026 · Main window: November 11–15 · About 10 minutes**

Three things worth discussing at the whiteboard:

- **Stop generating paragraphs when the product needs a decision.** Perplexity's new Decider models expose probabilities and extractive spans directly—but the attention design creates a subtle coupling between questions.
- **A weak actor can be a useful teacher.** A new agent paper separates choosing an action from judging its contribution after seeing what happened.
- **An image generator can become a perception model.** Open Vision Banana makes the recipe inspectable, including the surprisingly important problem of keeping class names attached to the right colors.

## 1. Decider v1.3: a classifier with a natural-language interface

*Rollout announced November 12*

Perplexity's 27B Decider and distilled 4B Flash model answer classification and scoring questions about shared context without autoregressively generating an explanation. The new **locate** operation extracts verbatim text with character offsets.

The engineering write-up is the interesting part. Generative backbones get bidirectional attention and a decision head. In v1.3, questions cannot directly attend to other questions, but the shared state attends to all questions. Consequently, **question isolation is not complete independence**: another question can still influence an answer through the state. Making the state fully cacheable costs quality on some tasks.

Perplexity reports 93.98% agreement with frontier-model reference answers on its production sample, versus 93.68% for v1.2. That is agreement with judges, not human-labeled accuracy; the sample is also dominated by a few task categories.

**For your retrieval pipeline:** this is a plausible candidate for relevance scoring, catalog entity matching or QA triage. Compare it with your cross-encoder and XGBoost baseline, including calibration and latency.

Availability needs care: the launch advertises Apache-licensed weights, but both model cards currently instruct users to authenticate for private-repository access. Cards are readable; unrestricted weight download was not verified. The Flash card estimates about 8.5 GiB for BF16 weights, before working memory.

Sources: [training, evaluation and attention design](https://www.perplexity.ai/en-GB/hub/blog/perplexity-decider) · [27B card](https://huggingface.co/perplexity-ai/pplx-decider-v1.3) · [Flash card](https://huggingface.co/perplexity-ai/pplx-decider-flash-v1.3)

## 2. Actor, Judge, or Teacher? Don't hire the judge by its acting audition

*Paper submitted November 12; HF discovery November 13*

An agent may know that a mug needs heating, yet place it on the table first because placement is the easier immediate subgoal. This paper asks whether the same model can recognize the useful heating action afterward.

Across ALFWorld experiments, the authors find that **context and question matter**: seeing the whole trajectory and asking whether an action made progress works better than asking whether the entire task will succeed.

Using JEV's action scores to supplement outcome-based GRPO raises a Qwen2.5-1.5B policy from **72.8% to 82.9% success on ALFWorld**, and **56.8% to 71.4% on WebShop**. The teacher disappears at inference.

The appealing idea is that retrospective judgment solves an easier information problem than prospective action selection. But these results do not establish a generally reliable judge: two environments, specific policies and teacher configurations, and hindsight that an online safety filter cannot access.

**My take:** screen proposed reward models on recorded trajectories before paying for RL. Ask whether their scores distinguish useful steps from no-ops, not merely successful episodes from failures.

Code and training scripts are public. This study evaluates earlier decision-model configurations, including Decider v1.2; it is not an independent validation of the new v1.3 release.

Sources: [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.17167v1) · [implementation and training scripts](https://github.com/ZJU-REAL/decision-agents)

## 3. SteerDistill: fix the quantized agent's failure context

*Submitted November 10; late discovery via HF's November 12 feed*

Quantization errors are not confined to slightly different logits. An agent may overlook a precondition, take a bad action and enter a state the full-precision model never visited.

SteerDistill finds a behavioral divergence, restores the pre-error context and lets a full-precision teacher guide the quantized student through retries. Execution feedback refines that guidance. Locally verified corrections are then distilled into the student, which runs without teacher guidance at inference.

Across three models and five agent benchmarks, the authors report **1.67–2.18 points above the strongest quantized baseline under W4A16**, with average scores within one point of BF16. W4A16 means four-bit weights and 16-bit activations.

**Why it matters:** calibration data should include the states your compressed policy actually creates. Matching a teacher on pristine trajectories can miss the exact failures that hurt deployment.

The recipe is not free: task-specific post-RL starting checkpoints, recoverable environment state, teacher supervision and local verification. A locally corrected action does not guarantee eventual success. Nor does recovered benchmark quality establish faster serving on your hardware.

Code, setup and evaluation guides are public; I did not verify downloadable task-trained checkpoints. The reference training setup uses eight A100 80GB GPUs, so this is a training method to study rather than a cheap weekend run.

Sources: [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.13410v1) · [code and reproduction requirements](https://github.com/air-embodied-brain/SteerDistill)

## 4. Open Vision Banana: perception as image editing, with the recipe exposed

*Submitted November 11; surfaced on HF November 13*

Ask an image editor to recolor every road pixel blue and every car pixel red. Decode the resulting colors, and image generation becomes segmentation. The same interface can encode depth or surface normals.

Open Vision Banana adapts 4B and 9B open-weight generators using public training data. The authors report **73.9 mIoU on zero-shot Cityscapes**, compared with Vision Banana's reported 69.9. This is a particular transfer result, not blanket superiority across perception tasks; instance segmentation remains a weaker area.

My favorite detail is **legend ensembling**: change the class-to-color mapping, repeat inference, map results back to classes and vote. This can correct unstable color bindings rather than merely averaging random noise. The paper reports gains up to 8.7 mIoU on Cityscapes, with additional inference cost.

**For shelf intelligence:** useful for investigating low-data segmentation or generating candidate annotations. Measure tiny-object boundaries and touching instances before trusting attractive qualitative outputs. A dense predictor's numeric output and an edited RGB visualization have different failure modes.

Code, data pipelines and checkpoint listings are public and inspectable. “Fully open recipe” should not be read as unrestricted commercial licensing: the main checkpoint card is labeled with FLUX noncommercial terms. Check the particular base and derivative you intend to use.

Sources: [paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.15844v1) · [code and recipe](https://github.com/OpenVisionBanana/OpenVisionBanana) · [checkpoints and license](https://huggingface.co/FunAILab/Open-Vision-Banana)

## 5. Gemini 4 Hydrogen: the worker model arrives alongside a router

*Announcement dated November 12, updated November 13*

After Wednesday's Carbon release, Google added a model aimed at faster everyday agent execution. Hydrogen has four thinking levels, a one-million-token context window and announced API pricing of $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens.

The more consequential addition may be **Orchestrator in Antigravity**, which switches models within a session. Google reports near-Carbon Terminal-Bench 4.0 success while consuming 24% less quota and completing 19% faster.

Treat that as a result for Google's model-plus-routing system. “Quota” is not necessarily dollars, and a benchmark average says little about your failure tail. Architecture and training details are not disclosed in the announcement.

**My take:** compare three policies: always use the stronger model, always use the faster model, and escalate after a defined failure signal. Keep retries and review time in the accounting. The router earns its place only if it improves the actual quality–latency–cost frontier.

For your coding-agent data workflow, unresolved validation errors could be one escalation signal. Self-reported confidence alone would be a weak gate. Broad API availability is announced; downloadable weights were not verified.

Source: [release and Orchestrator results](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-hydrogen/)

## 6. AlphaGenome 2: the same DNA can matter differently in different cells

*Announced November 11*

A DNA variant's effect can disappear inside a tissue average while mattering strongly in one cell type. AlphaGenome 2 moves toward cell-state-specific prediction, training on bulk and single-cell data including more than 107 million cell profiles.

The reusable ML idea is **conditioning on a new state representation**. Researchers can supply a single-cell gene-expression snapshot; the model compresses it into a conditioning representation and predicts variant effects without retraining for that experiment.

On the TenK10K immune-cell benchmark, DeepMind reports identifying causal variants at **30% versus 16%** for the comparison described in its announcement. That is a benchmark-specific recall claim, not discovery of twice as many clinically established disease causes.

**Why read outside your domain:** this is an interesting example of a specialized foundation model adapting through contextual measurements, rather than another round of fine-tuning for every new condition.

The model can prioritize experimental hypotheses; reproducing a known mechanism computationally does not replace prospective biological validation. Research API access is announced, with commercial research in private preview. The technical report says model source and weights will follow final publication. These are research predictions, not validated clinical diagnostics.

Sources: [announcement, conditioning and benchmark context](https://deepmind.google/blog/alphagenome-2-deciphering-how-cells-read-dna-to-better-understand-disease/) · [technical report](https://storage.googleapis.com/deepmind-media/DeepMind.com/Blog/alphagenome-2-deciphering-how-cells-read-dna-to-better-understand-disease/alphagenome-2.pdf)

## 7. XLA Serving Engine: read the benchmark table, not just the speedup

*Released November 5; late engineering discovery, not a new launch this week*

XLA Serving Engine exports model computation as StableHLO in an XLA SavedModel and serves it through a C++ runtime. Its natural territory is one-shot model inference, rather than long autoregressive generation.

Google's DLRM benchmark is unusually worth inspecting. On the same TPU VM, weights and wire protocol, under a **p99 ≤ 20 ms** target, XSE beats the strongest Python setup by **5× at one item per request**, **2× at 16**, and only **1.14× at 128**.

That shrinking gap is the story: batching amortizes Python and launch overhead. The benchmark also includes different host embedding implementations, so “removing Python” is not the only changed component.

**For your reranking or small-CV services:** profile preprocessing, dispatch, copies and concurrency before assuming the GPU model is the bottleneck. A faster kernel cannot rescue a service dominated by host overhead.

The export/runtime code and benchmark reproduction materials are public. Migration still depends on exportable operations and the actual deployment backend. The launch explicitly recommends vLLM or SGLang for large autoregressive LLMs. This is a useful serving option, not a universal replacement for your inference stack.

Sources: [launch and limitations](https://opensource.googleblog.com/2026/11/supercharging-xla-inference-xla-serving-engine.html) · [detailed benchmark](https://xla-serving.readthedocs.io/en/latest/benchmarks/dlrm_serving.html) · [code](https://github.com/openxla/xla-serving)

## 8. SGLang's consortium: a maintenance signal, not a speed claim

*Announced November 12*

LMSYS launched a consortium including NVIDIA, AMD, Intel, Google, Meta, Cloudflare, Modal and RadixArk. The stated focus includes model support, multimodal and reasoning workloads, distributed serving and hardware optimization.

The governance detail matters: members provide technical representatives, feedback and engineering resources, while maintainers retain technical decisions and code acceptance.

**My interpretation:** this could improve coordination around hardware support and production requirements. It does not make backend parity, reliability or future performance automatic. For a tech lead choosing a serving dependency, watch merged support, regression coverage and issue turnaround—not the number of logos.

This is an ecosystem development worth tracking, without an immediate reason to migrate a working deployment.

Source: [LMSYS announcement and governance](https://www.lmsys.org/blog/2026-11-12-sglang-consortium/)

The tension between question-conditioned representations, stable answers and cache reuse is directly relevant to retrieval/reranking services.

## One concrete experiment: does batching questions change your reranker?

Take **200 manually reviewed query–candidate pairs** from difficult product matches: same brand with different flavors, pack sizes, OCR truncation and absent-SKU cases.

With one decision model, ask three questions: “same product?”, “same pack size?” and “does the evidence contradict the match?” Compare:

- Each question asked alone.
- All three asked together.
- The same batch with questions and label order permuted.
- The batch plus an irrelevant question.

Keep each candidate's visual/OCR/catalog evidence identical. Compare with your existing reranker on the same labels.

Measure semantic answer flips, calibration, false acceptance at your target precision, latency and cost per pair. Report the automation coverage achieved at 98% precision, with uncertainty, rather than choosing an arbitrary score threshold.

**Hypothesis:** shared computation helps economics, but question-conditioned attention may create subtle cross-question effects. A low average flip rate can still hide unacceptable instability among near-duplicate SKUs. If it passes, you have a practical multi-attribute verifier; if it fails, separate scoring or a task-specific reranker may remain preferable.

This experiment needs no RL and tests a concrete production assumption: that changing the questions around a decision should not silently change the identity of the product.
